Every major AI lab is building agents that can shop for you. Most of the biggest retailers are blocking them. That is the central tension of agentic commerce in late 2026, and I think the blockers are making a mistake.

Amazon's Conditions of Use prohibit automated tools from accessing its platform without prior written approval. United Airlines terms forbid "any robot, spider, or other automatic device" from interacting with its site. Target, Best Buy, and Booking.com all maintain restrictive terms of service and bot-detection infrastructure aimed at keeping AI agents out.

These are massive transaction platforms, and they are actively fighting the thing that could drive their next wave of growth.

## The Amazon Paradox

Amazon is the clearest case of a company working against its own interests. On one side, Amazon has invested heavily in AI across its entire business — Alexa, AWS Bedrock, Amazon Q, generative product listings, AI-powered logistics. On the other side, it blocks the AI agents that would funnel more purchases through its platform.

The terms are explicit: no automated access without Amazon's written consent. And the enforcement backs it up — anyone who has tried to build an agent that touches Amazon knows the CAPTCHA walls and bot-detection are aggressive.

Why would a company that sells everything want to block the most efficient buyers?

I think it comes down to three things:

**Price transparency destroys margin.** An AI agent that comparison-shops across Amazon, Walmart, and Target in seconds puts constant downward pressure on prices. Amazon's retail margins are famously thin. If every purchase decision is mediated by an agent optimizing for the buyer, Amazon loses the impulse purchases, the "frequently bought together" upsells, and the Prime loyalty lock-in that make the economics work.

**Data leakage to competitors.** An AI agent scraping product listings, prices, availability, and reviews is building a structured dataset that any competitor can use. Amazon's product catalog is one of its deepest moats. Letting agents index it freely turns that moat into a public utility.

**Loss of the customer relationship.** If a shopper's AI agent handles the entire transaction — search, compare, purchase, track — Amazon becomes a fulfillment backend. The agent becomes the storefront. Amazon has spent years making sure customers start their shopping journey on Amazon. Agents threaten to move that starting point to ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini.

<Callout type="info">
Every platform blocking AI agents is making the same bet: the cost of losing control over the customer relationship outweighs the benefit of higher transaction volume. Whether that bet pays off depends on how fast the protocols catch up.
</Callout>

## The Airline and Travel Wall

United Airlines is one of the most explicit cases. Its terms prohibit automated access to the site for any purpose, and the enforcement has teeth — airlines have gone well beyond simple rate limiting with layered bot-detection on their booking flows.

This language is not new for airlines — scraper bans have existed for years. What has changed is the enforcement intensity. AI agents that can actually complete bookings are a different threat than the scrapers of five years ago.

The travel industry has a specific additional concern: rate parity. Hotels and OTAs have contractual obligations about where their best prices appear. An AI agent that surfaces a lower rate on an obscure booking site undermines those agreements. That makes the travel sector one of the places where AI agents could save consumers the most money — and one of the most aggressively locked down.

## Which Retailers Allow AI Shopping Agents

Not everyone is blocking agents. A few companies are betting that cooperating with AI agents is better than fighting them.

**Shopify** has been building commerce APIs for AI agents, extending Shop Pay into agentic checkout flows. Instead of agents scraping merchant sites, Shopify provides a structured integration path. The merchant keeps the customer data, the agent gets a reliable checkout flow. Over 100 million buyers already have Shop Pay accounts, which gives agents a large base of pre-authenticated buyers to work with.

**Walmart** has been more open than Amazon to structured agent access. Walmart's API ecosystem is more permissive, and the company appears to see agentic commerce as a differentiation play — if you cannot beat Amazon on the AI technology side, you can compete on the access side.

**Instacart** has explicitly partnered with AI assistants, offering structured APIs for product search, cart building, and checkout. For a delivery-first platform, AI agents are a natural fit — the agent handles the decision-making, Instacart handles the logistics.

## Agentic Commerce Protocols Compared

The blocking problem has spawned a wave of protocol proposals, each trying to create a standard way for AI agents to interact with commerce platforms. None of them are mature yet, but they represent the industry's best guess at how this gets resolved.

### ChatGPT Wallet and OpenAI's Dots

OpenAI has been building financial infrastructure for agents. The ChatGPT Wallet ties payment methods to the ChatGPT interface, and "Dots" — small autonomous payments — are designed to let agents make purchases on the user's behalf within pre-set spending limits.

The idea is explicit authorization: the user sets a budget and approves vendor categories, and the agent handles individual transactions within those guardrails.

<Callout type="warning">
OpenAI's commerce features are evolving rapidly. The payment infrastructure has been rolling out in stages, and the scope of what agents can actually purchase autonomously — and through which merchants — is still limited and changing. Check the current state before building on these capabilities.
</Callout>

### Google's Agent-to-Agent Protocol (A2A)

Google's A2A protocol, announced in April 2025, defines how AI agents communicate with each other. The commerce potential is significant: two agents — a buyer's and a seller's — could negotiate and transact through a structured message exchange.

Google has signed partners including PayPal, Visa, and Stripe. The protocol is open-source, which is Google's standard play: commoditize the protocol layer and compete on the intelligence layer above it.

### Visa's Intelligent Commerce

Visa has proposed its own framework for agentic payments. The Visa Intelligent Commerce initiative creates a tokenized payment layer where AI agents can carry pre-authorized payment credentials. The approach uses Visa's existing tokenization infrastructure to keep card details out of the agent's hands entirely.

Visa has an obvious infrastructure advantage here: it already sits between merchants and buyers on a massive scale. If Visa can make its agent-payment rails the default, it captures the agentic commerce transaction flow the same way it captured e-commerce.

### Mastercard's Agent Pay

Mastercard launched Agent Pay as a direct competitor to Visa's approach. The core mechanism is similar — tokenized credentials, pre-authorization, fraud monitoring — but Mastercard is emphasizing a biometric verification layer. The idea is that a human biometric confirmation (face or fingerprint) stands between the agent's intent and the actual charge above certain thresholds.

This is a smart design choice. People are more comfortable letting an AI agent spend their money if they know their fingerprint is still in the loop.

## The Trust Architecture Problem

Every protocol proposal is really trying to solve one problem: trust architecture. When a human shops, trust is bilateral — the buyer trusts the merchant, the merchant trusts the payment method. When an AI agent shops, trust becomes trilateral: the user trusts the agent, the merchant trusts the agent, and the payment network trusts the agent.

No existing commerce infrastructure was designed for this. Credit card networks assume a human cardholder. Merchant terms of service assume a human visitor. Fraud detection assumes human behavior patterns.

Building trust architecture for agents requires solving several problems simultaneously:

**Identity.** How does a merchant verify that an AI agent is acting on behalf of a specific, authorized human? OAuth tokens help, but they were designed for app-to-app delegation, not for autonomous agents making independent decisions.

**Authorization scope.** A human shopping on Amazon can buy anything Amazon sells. Should their agent have the same scope? Most proposals include spending limits and category restrictions, but the granularity varies widely.

**Liability.** If an AI agent buys the wrong thing, who is responsible? The user who set the parameters? The AI lab whose model made the decision? The merchant who accepted the transaction? The legal frameworks do not have answers yet, and no protocol can solve that alone.

**Auditability.** Regulators will eventually require that agentic transactions be auditable. Every purchase decision needs a traceable chain: what the user authorized, what the agent decided, what the merchant fulfilled. None of the current protocols have mature audit trail specifications.

<Callout type="tip">
If you are building anything in the agentic commerce space right now, the trust architecture is the thing to watch. The agent intelligence is already good enough. The payment rails, identity verification, and liability frameworks are where the real bottlenecks sit.
</Callout>

## What Happens Next for Agentic Commerce

The blocking strategy is a temporary equilibrium. It works while AI agents are a small fraction of traffic, but it breaks when agents become the primary way people interact with commerce.

Here is how I expect this to play out:

**Short term (next 6-12 months):** More retailers join the blocking camp. The few that cooperate — Shopify, Instacart, smaller merchants — gain disproportionate agent-driven traffic. Protocol proposals multiply, but none achieves critical mass.

**Medium term (1-2 years):** One or two protocols consolidate. My bet is on the payment network approach (Visa or Mastercard) winning over the AI-lab approach (OpenAI or Google), because the payment networks already have merchant relationships and regulatory standing. Amazon either launches its own agent API or watches Walmart and Shopify eat its agent-driven commerce share.

**Long term (3-5 years):** Agentic commerce becomes a standard channel alongside web, mobile, and voice. The retailers that blocked agents early will have lost years of agent-channel optimization. The ones that cooperated early will have the data, the integrations, and the customer trust to dominate the channel.

The companies blocking AI agents today are not wrong about the risks. I think they are wrong about the timeline. The shift to agent-mediated commerce is already underway. The retailers building the rails now will own the channel. The ones fighting it will eventually build the same rails — just later, and from behind.
