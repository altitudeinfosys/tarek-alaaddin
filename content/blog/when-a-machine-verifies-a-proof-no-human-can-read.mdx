Seventeen million lines of code. A four-page personal note. No traditional paper. That is the proof of the Riemann Hypothesis — perhaps the most important unsolved problem in mathematics for 167 years — as released by Levent Alpöge on November 20, 2026.

The note says what he believes. The Lean library says why. There is no sixty-page exposition, no arxiv preprint, no human-readable walkthrough of the argument. The proof itself exists only in the language of a proof assistant.

By the next day, independent mathematicians had confirmed it correct.

## What Actually Happened

Alpöge, a number theorist on Anthropic's staff, [posted a brief note](https://alpo.ge/remark.pdf) on his personal website announcing that, working with Claude, he had found a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis. The note is four pages — it describes what he believes was proved and how the work was done, but it does not contain the proof itself. The proof is Lean code: 175,187 modules totaling roughly 17 million lines, released as 14 download files on his site.

By the end of the same day, MIT mathematician Andrew Sutherland had independently compiled the entire codebase and [reported to Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/the-ai-riemann-hypothesis-proof-is-almost-certainly-true-independent-mathematicians-attest/) that it rested on only "the three standard axioms" and that the chance of anything being "fishy" was "approximately zero."

<Callout type="info">
**What the three standard axioms are.** Every Lean proof ultimately rests on `propext` (propositional extensionality), `Classical.choice` (the axiom of choice), and `Quot.sound` (quotient soundness). These are the logical foundations baked into Lean's type theory. The fact that Alpöge's proof uses nothing beyond these means no hidden assumptions were smuggled in.
</Callout>

## The Verification Blitz

What happened next was unprecedented. The Lean and Mathlib communities mobilized to run the proof through every independent checker they could find.

On November 21, they published [a joint statement](https://leanprover-community.github.io/blog/posts/riemann-hypothesis/) — endorsed by the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative — declaring "full confidence" that the proof is correct.

Their certification rested on four criteria, originally outlined by Imperial College London mathematician Kevin Buzzard:

1. **The statement is correct.** The Lean code proves Mathlib's own `RiemannHypothesis` definition, which had been carefully vetted by experts in both Lean and number theory long before this proof arrived.

2. **The code compiles.** Dozens of people independently compiled the full codebase with zero errors.

3. **The code proves the right thing.** Two independent tools — `comparator` and `con-leche --compare` — confirmed that the theorem in Alpöge's library is exactly Mathlib's statement of the Riemann Hypothesis. This matters because Lean checks whatever statement it is given; a proof of a *different* theorem would also compile.

4. **The proof is valid.** Multiple independent proof checkers accepted the exported proof: Lean's official kernel, `con-leche`, `con-ron` (a Rust port), `nanoda` (an independent Rust kernel), and `lean4lean`. The checkers `con-leche` and `con-ron` come with their own formal proof that they cannot accept a false proof, addressing concerns that Lean's logic itself might have exploitable flaws.

Sutherland's [verification page at MIT](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/) — last updated November 22 — documents every check with logs, scripts, and hashes so anyone can reproduce the results. Michael Rubinstein at the University of Waterloo ran an independent verification using Claude Code on university compute servers, also documenting the full process.

## What Was Actually Proved

This is where precision matters, and where most press coverage got sloppy.

Alpöge's proof establishes five formal theorems:

1. **The Riemann Hypothesis** — every non-trivial zero of the Riemann zeta function has real part 1/2.
2. The same statement in the submission's own notation (Lean accepts both as definitionally equal).
3. **No zeros to the right of the critical line for all Dirichlet L-functions** — for every modulus N ≥ 1 and every Dirichlet character χ.
4. **Zeros in the critical strip lie on the critical line** for all Dirichlet L-functions.
5. The same for primitive characters — the standard formulation of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis for Dirichlet L-functions.

That scope is enormous. It covers all Dirichlet L-functions across all moduli and all characters.

<Callout type="warning">
**What it does NOT prove.** The term "Generalized Riemann Hypothesis" can refer to several different things. Alpöge's proof covers GRH for Dirichlet L-functions specifically. It does not cover Dedekind zeta functions, Hecke L-functions, or automorphic L-functions in general — the Extended Riemann Hypothesis (ERH). Some early headlines overstated the scope. Sutherland's verification page is explicit about this boundary.
</Callout>

## The Arithmetic Consequence Test

As a clever sanity check, Sutherland's team combined the proof with an independent formalization of Robin's and Lagarias's criteria — two inequalities about divisor sums that are known to be equivalent to the Riemann Hypothesis. The combined code produced both theorems with no hypotheses and no mention of the zeta function at all.

Robin's inequality states that for all n > 5040, the sum of divisors σ(n) is strictly less than e^γ · n · ln(ln(n)), where γ is Euler's constant. Lagarias's inequality gives an upper bound involving harmonic numbers. Both are now formally proved, as consequences of Alpöge's work combined with the Robin1984 formalization.

This is elegant because it tests the proof against an entirely different mathematical formulation, one that makes claims about concrete integers rather than complex analysis.

## Claude's Role — And What It Isn't

Alpöge described the work as done "working with Claude." Sutherland ran his verification checks with Claude Code. Rubinstein used Claude Code for his independent verification at Waterloo.

It is tempting to frame this as "AI proves the Riemann Hypothesis." That framing is wrong in important ways.

Alpöge is a professional mathematician. He guided the conceptual direction. Claude helped produce the formalization. The collaboration was real, but the mathematical insight came from a human mathematician working with an AI tool — not from an AI acting autonomously.

The distinction matters for the Clay Mathematics Institute's Millennium Prize consideration. It matters for how we think about mathematical authorship. And it matters for accurately understanding what AI systems can and cannot do.

Terence Tao — himself a Fields Medalist — [described the proof on his blog](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/11/20/unforced-euler-singularity-unforced-navier-stokes-singularity-and-the-generalized-riemann-hypothesis/) as "enormous" and noted that Alpöge used an internal AI model. That is the right frame: a mathematician who used a powerful tool to accomplish something neither could have done alone.

## The No-Paper Problem

Here is the genuinely uncomfortable part: nobody has read the 17 million lines that constitute this proof.

Not "nobody has verified it." Verification is thorough and ongoing. The Lean and Mathlib communities are confident. Multiple independent kernel checkers accept it. The statement is correct. The axioms are clean.

But no human being has traced the full argument line by line. There is no published paper walking through the key ideas, the novel techniques, or the mathematical intuition behind the proof. Work to explain it has begun — more on that below — but 17 million lines of machine-checked logic is a lot of ground to cover.

The Lean and Mathlib joint statement ends with a telling line: they look forward to "all the activity in the mathematical community that will help explain the new ideas in this proof."

Rubinstein at Waterloo has started working on this. He published a [164-page working draft](https://www.math.uwaterloo.ca/~mrubinst/alpoge_claude_grh/zeta-paper.pdf) explaining the proof's approach for the Riemann zeta function specifically — a readable explanation of the key ideas, albeit still a work in progress.

This is a new kind of problem for mathematics. We have a proof that is almost certainly correct. We know what it proves. We do not yet know how it proves it, in any human sense of "know."

## Why Formal Verification Changes Everything

The traditional path for a major mathematical result goes: preprint, seminar talks, peer review, journal publication, years of community scrutiny. Major proofs have historically taken years to validate through human review — the community reads, questions, re-derives, and eventually accepts or rejects.

The core verification of Alpöge's proof took a day.

That speed is not because the mathematics is simpler. It is because formal verification sidesteps the bottleneck of human review. A Lean proof that compiles, states the right theorem, and passes independent kernel checkers is correct — not in the hand-wavy sense of "probably correct," but in the mathematical sense that the logical steps follow from the axioms. As the verification blitz showed, compilation alone is not enough — you also need to confirm the statement matches what you think was proved and that independent checkers accept the exported proof. All of those checks passed here.

This does not replace understanding. The mathematical community still wants to know what techniques Alpöge used, what new ideas are in the proof, and what other problems those ideas might solve. But it does change the relationship between certainty and understanding.

We now live in a world where a mathematical result can be verified before it is understood.

## The Prize Question

The Riemann Hypothesis is one of the Clay Mathematics Institute's seven Millennium Prize Problems, carrying a $1 million award. Clay's rules are deliberate: the institute does not accept solutions sent to it directly. The standard process requires publication in a refereed journal, followed by a two-year waiting period during which the broader community assesses the work.

No traditional paper has been submitted. The proof exists only as Lean code. How the Clay Institute will navigate this situation — a formally verified proof with no human-readable paper — is itself an open question.

What is not in question: the formal verification is about as thorough as it gets. The prize process, however, was designed for a world where proofs were written in natural language and evaluated by human reviewers. The process will catch up. The result is not going away.

## What This Means for the Rest of Us

If you work in software, the Lean verification story is familiar in structure. We already trust compilers more than we trust code review for catching certain classes of bugs. We already accept that a type system can verify properties that manual testing cannot.

Formal proof verification is the mathematical equivalent. The question is not whether machine-checked proofs are reliable — they are, and this episode demonstrates it dramatically. The question is what happens to fields built on human understanding when the proofs outrun the humans.

Take the framing accordingly — but the verification results come from independent mathematicians at MIT, Waterloo, Imperial College London, and the broader Lean community, none of whom work for Anthropic.

For AI specifically, this is not a story about artificial general intelligence or machines replacing mathematicians. It is a story about a mathematician who was more productive with an AI collaborator than without one. The same pattern is playing out in software engineering, in scientific research, in legal analysis — anywhere that rigorous reasoning meets large problem spaces.

The interesting question going forward is not whether more proofs will be AI-assisted. They will. The interesting question is whether the mathematical community will develop new norms for understanding proofs that exist only in machine-checkable form — or whether understanding will simply become optional once verification is reliable enough.

Since 1859, the Riemann Hypothesis stood as an open challenge. As of this week, it stands as a verified theorem — awaiting human comprehension.
