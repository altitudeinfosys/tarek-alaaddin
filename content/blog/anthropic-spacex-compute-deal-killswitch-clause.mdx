On May 6, 2026, two companies that publicly despised each other signed one of the largest AI compute deals of the year. Anthropic — the company Elon Musk once called "evil" and accused of hating Western civilization — just leased the entire capacity of SpaceX's Colossus 1 data center in Memphis. Over 220,000 NVIDIA GPUs. More than 300 megawatts of power. Available within weeks.

Buried in the deal is something nobody in AI infrastructure has tried before: a killswitch clause. SpaceX reserves the right to reclaim every GPU if Anthropic's AI engages in actions that harm humanity.

That single provision makes this deal more interesting than every hyperscaler contract Anthropic has signed combined.

## What the Deal Actually Includes

The numbers are straightforward. Anthropic gets exclusive access to Colossus 1 in Memphis, Tennessee — the data center SpaceX inherited when it absorbed xAI earlier this year. The facility houses over 220,000 NVIDIA GPUs (a mix of H100, H200, and GB200 accelerators) with more than 300 megawatts of power capacity.

SpaceXAI — the rebranded AI division after Musk dissolved xAI as a standalone company on May 7, 2026 — has already migrated its own training workloads to the newer Colossus 2 facility, freeing Colossus 1 entirely for Anthropic's use.

The capacity comes online within a month. That timeline matters because most of Anthropic's other infrastructure deals are measured in quarters and years, not weeks.

<Callout type="info">
For immediate context: Anthropic simultaneously announced doubled rate limits for Claude Code across Pro, Max, Team, and Enterprise plans. Peak-hours throttling was removed for Pro and Max accounts, and Opus API rate limits were raised considerably. The SpaceX compute almost certainly explains why those limits could move so fast.
</Callout>

## Why Musk Changed His Mind

The about-face requires explanation. In February 2026, Musk publicly wrote that Anthropic "hates Western Civilization." He'd previously called the company "misanthropic" and "evil." He accused them of stealing data at massive scale.

Then he spent time with the team.

According to Musk's own statements, he met with senior Anthropic leadership to understand their approach to ensuring Claude benefits humanity. He came away saying that "no one set off my evil detector" and described the team as highly competent people who cared about doing the right thing. He added that "so long as they engage in critical self-examination, Claude will probably be good."

The shift has a financial dimension too. SpaceX is targeting an IPO in mid-2026 with valuations discussed in the $1.75 to $2 trillion range. Leasing underutilized compute to one of the most valuable private AI companies on Earth strengthens that narrative considerably. Anthropic, valued between $900 billion and $1.2 trillion in secondary markets, is a tenant that makes SpaceX's infrastructure play look credible.

## The Killswitch Clause

Here's what makes the deal structurally novel. SpaceX reserved the right to withdraw compute resources if Anthropic's AI engages in actions that harm humanity.

This isn't standard cloud provider language. AWS doesn't include clauses about reclaiming servers if your models misbehave. Google Cloud doesn't reserve the right to cut your training runs based on safety assessments. This is Musk inserting a unilateral safety valve into an infrastructure lease.

The practical questions are obvious. Who decides what "harm to humanity" means? What's the threshold? Is this a board-level decision at SpaceX, or does Musk himself hold the trigger? What happens to in-progress training runs? How much notice does Anthropic get?

None of these details are public. The ambiguity is the point — the clause creates a credible but undefined threat, which gives SpaceX leverage without binding it to specific criteria that could be gamed or litigated around.

<Callout type="warning">
The killswitch clause exists in a broader context. In April 2026, Anthropic filed a brief with a federal appeals court arguing it has no visibility, technical ability, or "kill switch" for its technology once deployed — in the context of classified settings. Anthropic was arguing it can't remotely disable deployed models. SpaceX's clause addresses a different layer: it controls the compute infrastructure, not the deployed models. The distinction matters.
</Callout>

## What This Says About AI Safety Governance

The timing of this deal collides with a turbulent period in Anthropic's safety story.

In February 2026, Anthropic released version 3.0 of its Responsible Scaling Policy. The update removed the previous commitment to pause training if model capabilities outpaced the company's ability to ensure safety. The old policy was a hard commitment — if we can't prove it's safe, we don't ship it. The new version replaced that with "nonbinding but publicly-declared targets" that Anthropic would grade itself against.

The catalyst was direct pressure from the Pentagon. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth gave Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei a Friday deadline to roll back safeguards or risk losing a $200 million Pentagon contract and being placed on what effectively amounts to a government blacklist.

Anthropic's chief science officer Jared Kaplan framed the change this way: responsible AI developers pausing growth while less careful actors continued wouldn't make the world safer. The logic is coherent but represents a fundamental shift — from hard safety constraints to aspirational safety goals.

Now SpaceX has inserted itself as an external safety check. The killswitch clause means there's at least one entity with both the power and stated willingness to yank Anthropic's compute if things go sideways. Whether Musk would actually pull that trigger is debatable. That the clause exists at all is the significant part.

## Anthropic's Compute Empire

The SpaceX deal doesn't exist in isolation. Anthropic has been on an infrastructure spending spree that dwarfs anything the AI industry has seen from a single company.

Here's the full picture:

- **Amazon**: Up to 5 gigawatts of capacity, with nearly 1GW of Trainium2 and Trainium3 coming online by end of 2026. Amazon invested an additional $25 billion (building on $8 billion previously), and Anthropic committed over $100 billion to AWS over 10 years.
- **Google/Broadcom**: 5 gigawatts beginning in 2027.
- **Microsoft/NVIDIA**: $30 billion in Azure capacity through a strategic partnership.
- **FluidStack**: Part of Anthropic's $50 billion US AI infrastructure commitment.
- **SpaceX**: 300+ MW immediately via Colossus 1, with expressed interest in developing multiple gigawatts of orbital compute.

Amazon and Google alone account for over 10 gigawatts of committed capacity, with Microsoft, FluidStack, and SpaceX adding substantially more on top.

The SpaceX deal stands out not for its size — 300MW is a fraction of the Amazon commitment — but for its speed. Anthropic admitted that demand for Claude has caused "inevitable strain on infrastructure" affecting reliability and performance during peak hours. Most of the deals listed above deliver capacity over quarters and years. SpaceX delivers within a month.

<Callout type="info">
The immediate capacity explains a lot. Anthropic reported that Claude usage grew 80x in a single recent quarter. When your demand curve looks like that, a 5-gigawatt deal arriving in 2027 doesn't help you today. A 300MW facility available in weeks does.
</Callout>

## The Orbital Data Center Angle

The announcement included an understated bombshell: Anthropic expressed interest in partnering with SpaceX to develop multiple gigawatts of orbital AI compute capacity.

SpaceX has been laying groundwork for space-based data centers for over a year. In January 2026, the company filed plans with the FCC for a massive satellite constellation that could eventually generate 100 gigawatts of AI compute capacity by launching one million tonnes of satellites annually. The satellites would operate between 500km and 2,000km altitude in sun-synchronous orbits to maximize solar power collection, connected to the Starlink network via high-bandwidth optical links.

SpaceX plans to begin pilot testing on-orbit compute nodes on Starlink V3 hardware in 2026, with each Starship launch carrying approximately 60 high-capacity satellites.

Is this realistic on a meaningful timeline? Probably not within this decade. Space-based data centers at any meaningful scale look like they're a decade away at minimum. But the expressed interest from Anthropic gives SpaceX's orbital compute narrative a named customer with a credible need for massive capacity — and that's valuable for an IPO prospectus.

## The Strange Bedfellows Problem

The deal highlights how compute scarcity creates alliances that ideology alone would never produce.

Musk owns the lawsuit against OpenAI and has been one of the loudest critics of how AI companies operate. Anthropic was founded by people who left OpenAI over safety disagreements. Both entities share a common adversary in Sam Altman, but their philosophies about AI safety, deployment, and governance diverge significantly.

What united them is straightforward: Anthropic needed GPUs immediately, and SpaceX had GPUs sitting idle. The rest is rationalization after the fact.

This pattern is repeating across the industry. The $700 billion that big tech is projected to spend on AI infrastructure in 2026 is creating relationships that would have been unthinkable two years ago. When the alternative to partnering with a controversial figure is telling paying customers their API calls will fail during peak hours, the calculus gets simple fast.

## Community Backlash and Data Center Opposition

The deal also lands against a backdrop of growing public resistance to AI data center construction. A recent Fortune investigation documented conspiracy theories spreading through community opposition groups — posts labeling AI facilities as surveillance centers and population control tools.

But beneath the conspiracy noise, there are legitimate grievances: transparency gaps in permitting, land-use conflicts, electricity demand straining local grids, and water consumption for cooling. A Michigan township voted against an OpenAI-Oracle data center project, only to have the developer successfully sue; construction began less than two months after the rejection.

The Memphis facility Anthropic is leasing already exists, so it sidesteps the construction controversy. But as Anthropic's compute appetite grows to the multi-gigawatt scale described above, the siting and permitting battles will become a material business risk — not just a PR headache.

## What to Watch

Three things will determine whether this deal becomes a template or an anomaly:

**The killswitch in practice.** If Anthropic ships a model that generates genuine controversy — and controversy is guaranteed given the pace of capability advancement — does SpaceX invoke the clause? Or is it decorative language that never gets tested? The first time it gets close to being triggered will reveal whether this is a real governance mechanism or a PR provision.

**Orbital compute timeline.** The space-based data center narrative is compelling but currently more IPO-friendly than technically mature. If SpaceX's pilot tests on Starlink V3 produce real benchmarks in late 2026, the expressed interest from Anthropic could convert into a binding agreement. If the pilots stall, the orbital angle fades into a footnote.

**The safety policy gap.** Anthropic removed its hard commitment to pause training if safety couldn't be guaranteed, replacing it with aspirational public targets. SpaceX added an external killswitch on compute. These two developments point in opposite directions — one loosening internal constraints, the other imposing external ones. How that tension resolves will shape the governance model for frontier AI development more broadly.

The Anthropic-SpaceX deal is ultimately a story about what happens when the demand for AI compute exceeds the capacity of normal business relationships to supply it. Companies that called each other evil are now sharing data centers. Infrastructure providers are inserting safety clauses into lease agreements. And somewhere in orbit, the next generation of AI compute is being sketched on a whiteboard at SpaceX.

The boring interpretation is that this is just a real estate deal with marketing language attached. The interesting interpretation is that a killswitch clause in an infrastructure lease might become the most consequential AI safety mechanism that nobody at any AI lab designed.
