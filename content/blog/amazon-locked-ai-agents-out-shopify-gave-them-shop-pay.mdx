On October 19, I wrote about [Amazon and Reddit locking out AI agents](/blog/amazon-reddit-blocking-ai-agents). The punchline was simple: your agent can negotiate your cable bill but it cannot buy toilet paper on Amazon.

That piece covered the lockout. This one covers what happened in the ten days since — because while Amazon was building walls, the rest of retail was building doors.

## Amazon's Agent Lockout: The Recap

Amazon started blocking AI agents in September 2026. Meta's Muse agent was first, cut off on September 20 after Amazon asked Meta to remove Amazon from Muse's shopping capabilities and Meta declined. By mid-October, Grok Bot users were posting screenshots of rejection messages. A Jefferies analysis found roughly one-third of tested US websites now block Meta's Muse agent.

Amazon's Conditions of Use, updated August 14, 2026, added an "Agents" section: agents must identify themselves in the user-agent string, must not imitate human typing or bypass CAPTCHAs, and must answer truthfully if asked whether they are a computer. Amazon reserves the right to limit any agent "at our sole discretion."

The message to agents was clear: not here, not now, not unless we say so.

## Shopify Turns On Agentic Commerce

While Amazon was blocking agents, Shopify was writing them checks.

On September 21, 2026, Shopify turned on agent checkout by default for its roughly one million merchants. That was not an opt-in experiment. It was a structural change to how Shopify commerce works. Every eligible Shopify storefront became a place where AI agents could browse products, add items to a cart, and complete a purchase — using structured APIs, not screen scraping.

The technical foundation is WebMCP, which Shopify's Gil Greenberg announced on September 28. WebMCP gives browser-based agents a structured way to read a checkout page, update it, and submit it with the buyer's authorization. Before WebMCP, agents that wanted to buy something on a Shopify store had to navigate HTML like a human. After WebMCP, they speak a commerce protocol.

<Callout type="info">
Shopify calls the protocol UCP — Universal Commerce Protocol. Greenberg described it as "the shared language that hosted MCPs and WebMCP both speak to." It is the plumbing that lets an agent built by Meta interact with a store built on Shopify without either side writing custom integration code.
</Callout>

Then came Shop MCP on October 13 — a way for MCP-compatible agents to search and browse Shopify's product catalog without even opening a browser. And on October 28, Shopify shipped its Fall '26 Edition, codenamed "Exponential," which bundled the whole agentic commerce stack into one announcement: agents as a first-class sales channel.

## Shop Pay for AI Agents: The Payment Rail

This is the part that matters most. The biggest objection to agent commerce has always been payments. How does a bot pay? Whose card is on file? What happens when the bot buys the wrong thing?

Shopify's answer is Shop Pay — the same one-click checkout that more than 400 million people already trust, according to Shopify's Fall '26 announcement. When an AI agent checks out on a Shopify store using Shop Pay, the buyer's card details are never shared with the agent or the store. The agent initiates the purchase. The buyer approves it. Their actual card number never leaves Shop Pay's infrastructure.

The agents that currently support Shop Pay checkout include Meta's Muse, Anthropic's Claude Duet, and xAI's Grok Bot — that last one confirmed directly on Shopify's Fall '26 Editions page. Noah Shinn's Instinct agent also announced Shop Pay support in late September, describing the flow as: the agent "sets up your order with the store," and the buyer checks out with Shop Pay using saved payment and shipping details.

This design solves the trust problem at the protocol level. The agent never sees your card number. And the buyer retains approval authority over every transaction.

<Callout type="warning">
Shop Pay's "card never shared with the agent" model is specific to Shopify stores. When Grok Bot buys from a non-Shopify merchant, it uses what xAI calls a "one-time Shop card" — a separate payment credential for that purchase. The goal is the same (keep the buyer's real card away from the agent), but the implementation differs.
</Callout>

## Grok Bot's Shopping Stack: Three Releases in Five Days

While Shopify was building the rails, Grok Bot was building the train.

Over the last two weeks, xAI shipped a rapid sequence of Grok Bot updates that turned it from a general-purpose agent into something that looks a lot like a dedicated shopping assistant:

- **v0.75.2 (October 22):** Bots can pay with Shop Pay on Shopify stores and use a one-time Shop card everywhere else.
- **v0.77.0 (October 26):** A new Vault Payments tab for managing payment methods. Two new templates: Order Concierge (tracks all your orders across stores) and Deal Watcher (monitors prices and alerts you to drops).
- **v0.78.1 (October 27):** Product cards with photos, ratings, price, and store name. A carousel for browsing multiple results. A "Buy now" button that requires explicit buyer approval before any purchase. Subscription templates for recurring tasks like try-and-cancel free trials, finding unused subscriptions, and watching prices over time.

That is three releases in five days. Eric Zakariasson confirmed v0.78.1 was stable as of October 28.

The speed is notable. Amazon blocks Grok Bot. Within two weeks, Grok Bot has a full shopping interface with product cards, payment management, and recurring purchase templates — all routing through merchants that actually want agent traffic.

## Amazon vs. Shopify: Two Strategies for Agent Commerce

Amazon and Shopify are both responding to the same reality: AI agents are becoming a meaningful traffic source for online retail. They just drew opposite conclusions about what to do about it.

**Amazon's bet:** Control the marketplace. If agents are going to shop, they will do it on Amazon's terms, through Amazon's APIs, with Amazon's permission. Amazon has the demand-side leverage (customers are already there) and the logistics network to make this work. Blocking agents now is a negotiating position, not a permanent stance. When Amazon does open up, it will be on Amazon's infrastructure.

**Shopify's bet:** Own the payment rail. If agents are going to shop everywhere, give them a way to pay that keeps the buyer safe and the merchant happy. Shopify does not care which agent you use or which store you visit — it cares that Shop Pay is how the transaction settles on Shopify stores. The more agents that route purchases through Shop Pay, the more Shopify captures.

These are not equally risky strategies. Amazon risks losing transactions to merchants who do accept agents. Shopify risks building infrastructure for a market that may not materialize at the scale it needs.

## The Numbers (and the Gaps)

Here is what we can verify:

- **400 million+** Shop Pay users (Shopify's own figure, from the Fall '26 Edition announcement)
- **47+** bots blocked by Amazon (per prior reporting)
- **~1 million** Shopify merchants with agent checkout enabled by default
- Grok Bot, Muse, Claude Duet, and Instinct all confirmed as Shop Pay-compatible agents

Here is what we cannot verify:

- Grok Bot's actual daily active users. An unofficial estimate of roughly 600,000 DAU circulated on X, attributed to @Napoleon__Bon. xAI has not confirmed any usage numbers.
- Whether Amazon's blocking is permanent or a prelude to launching its own agent API. Amazon has made no public statement about its agent strategy beyond the Conditions of Use update.
- The volume of agent-initiated purchases on Shopify. Neither Shopify nor any agent maker has released transaction data.

## What This Means for Builders

If you are building an AI agent that buys things, the landscape just split in two.

**The Shopify path is open now.** WebMCP, Shop MCP, and the UCP protocol mean you can build an agent that searches products, browses stores, and completes checkout on any eligible Shopify merchant — today. Shop Pay handles the payment. The buyer approves every purchase. Your agent never touches card credentials.

**The Amazon path is closed until further notice.** Amazon's Conditions of Use make unauthorized agent access a violation. Building an agent that scrapes Amazon or impersonates a human browser is a terms-of-service violation that will get your users blocked.

**The rest of retail is fragmented.** As I covered in the [October 19 post](/blog/amazon-reddit-blocking-ai-agents), some merchants welcome agents — Walmart has a Muse partnership, Best Buy and Sephora have similar deals. Others block them outright. There is no universal standard for which stores accept agent traffic and which do not.

The pragmatic move for agent builders right now is to route commerce through Shopify's stack. Amazon will likely open up eventually — a company that dominates online retail is not going to watch agent-driven purchases flow elsewhere forever. But until Amazon ships its own agent program, Shopify is the only platform offering a complete agent-to-checkout pipeline with a real payment rail.

## Platforms vs. Protocols: The Bigger Picture for Agentic Commerce

The agent commerce split is not really about Amazon versus Shopify. It is about two models of how the internet works when AI agents become a significant share of traffic.

In one model, platforms control access. They decide which agents get in, what those agents can do, and how transactions work. This is the Amazon model, and it is also the model that Delta, Yelp, and eBay are following.

In the other model, protocols enable access. Any agent that speaks the right protocol can interact with any merchant that supports it. The platform's job is to make the protocol work, not to gatekeep who uses it. This is the Shopify model.

History suggests both models will coexist. The web has always had walled gardens and open protocols running side by side. But the ratio between them matters enormously for what kind of agents get built and what they can do.

If most commerce flows through gated platforms, agents become integrations — approved, permissioned, limited. If most commerce flows through open protocols, agents become browsers — general-purpose, permissionless, powerful.

My bet: Shopify is on the right side of this. Not because Amazon is wrong to be cautious, but because the history of the internet consistently rewards the platform that makes access easier over the one that makes access harder. Amazon will open up — it has to. But by the time it does, Shop Pay may already be the default way agents pay for things. And defaults, once set, are very hard to undo.
