Elon Musk described a calling capability for Grok Bot on October 25 — the bot calls people and businesses on your behalf. The official Grok Bot changelog, updated the day before, does not mention calling. Those two facts exist at the same time, and SpaceXSI has not publicly reconciled them.

This post separates what is confirmed from what is not, lists the legal and trust questions that remain wide open, and explains why the gap matters more than the feature itself.

## The Musk Post

On October 25, 2026 at 14:35 UTC, Musk posted on X describing Grok Bot's calling capability. The description: the bot calls a person or business, makes a request, takes notes during the conversation, and sends you a summary afterward.

That is a four-step workflow — initiate, request, listen, summarize. It is specific enough to sound like a shipped feature. But Musk's post alone does not mean the feature has reached stable users — and in this case, the changelog tells a different story.

## What Prerelease Builds Show

The day before Musk's post, build watchers were already seeing phone-related UI elements in prerelease versions of Grok Bot.

On October 24, @blankspeaker documented builds 0.77.0-pre.3 through 0.77.0-pre.19, noting a calls panel, an on-hold status indicator, and a pressed-keys readout — the kind of DTMF interface you would need to navigate an automated phone menu. That same day, @alexmichaelio published a roundup post that accumulated over 715,000 views, summarizing the prerelease sightings.

The prerelease evidence is consistent with a calling feature being built. It is not evidence that the feature shipped to stable users.

## What the Official Changelog Actually Says

The Grok Bot changelog at x.ai/changelog/bot lists v0.75.8 as the latest release, dated October 24, 2026. That entry covers auto-review rule cards and smarter connectors. It does not mention phone calls, voice calls to external numbers, or any calling-related feature.

There is no v0.77 entry in the changelog at all. The prerelease builds that show calling UI are running well ahead of the published stable version.

<Callout type="warning">
Prerelease builds often run ahead of stable releases. But a CEO announcement running ahead of the changelog is different — it means there is no official release note, no documentation, and no changelog entry backing the claim that this feature is available today.
</Callout>

## No Documentation Page Exists

As of October 25, the Grok Bot documentation at docs.x.ai does not include an outbound calling page. There is no guide for setting up phone calls, no list of supported countries, no FAQ about how calls work. The feature Musk described has zero official documentation.

Compare this to how SpaceXSI has handled other sensitive features. The @bot tagging feature, for instance, has a documentation page that covers how tagged requests work. Phone calling has received none of that treatment.

## Four Things That Are Not Documented

Even if you accept Musk's post as confirmation that calling exists in some form, four critical questions have no public answer:

**1. AI Disclosure.** Does the bot tell the person on the other end that they are talking to an AI? No statement from SpaceXSI addresses this. Google Duplex, which has been making restaurant reservations since 2018, documents both disclosure and recording practices. Google's business-calling feature in Search, launched in July 2025, includes disclosure as well. SpaceXSI has not said whether Grok Bot follows this precedent.

**2. Recording.** Are calls recorded? If so, by whom, and who has access? Call recording laws vary by jurisdiction — some states require all-party consent, meaning both sides must agree. SpaceXSI has published no recording policy for Grok Bot calls.

**3. Supported Countries.** Which countries and regions can use the feature? Phone regulations differ dramatically across jurisdictions — automated calling rules vary widely. SpaceXSI has not published a supported-countries list.

**4. Plan Eligibility.** Which subscription tier includes calling? There is no published information about which plans include phone calls or whether the feature is available to all users.

## The Regulatory Framework Already Covers AI Voice

The regulatory framework around AI-initiated phone calls is clearer than SpaceXSI's silence suggests.

In February 2024, the FCC issued ruling FCC 24-17, which determined that AI-generated voices fall under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act's existing rules for artificial and prerecorded voices. The ruling was issued in response to concerns about deepfake robocalls.

The TCPA's artificial-voice rules carry enforcement mechanisms that make them hard to ignore. How exactly those rules apply to a user-directed AI assistant calling on someone's behalf is a question that has not been tested. But the FCC ruling makes clear that AI-generated voices are not exempt from the framework.

<Callout type="info">
FCC ruling FCC 24-17 (February 2024) placed AI-generated voices under the same TCPA rules that govern robocalls. The ruling was issued in response to deepfake robocall concerns and makes clear that AI voice technology is not exempt from existing telephone consumer protection rules.
</Callout>

## What Google Did Differently

Google is the closest precedent for AI phone calls at consumer scale, and their approach to disclosure and documentation is instructive.

Google Duplex launched in 2018 as a system that calls restaurants to make reservations. Google has documented both disclosure and recording practices for the service. When Google expanded calling to Search in July 2025, disclosure was part of the documented feature set.

Google has also faced a legal challenge on a related front. In October 2026, a lawsuit was filed against Google alleging that its Cloud Contact Center AI recorded customer service calls without adequate disclosure, potentially violating wiretapping laws. That is a different product and a different use case, but it shows that even companies with documented AI calling practices are not immune to legal action.

The point is not that Google's approach is flawless. The point is that Google published documentation and addressed disclosure. SpaceXSI has done neither for Grok Bot's calling feature.

## The Third-Party Angle: Bland AI

In mid-to-late October, Bland AI — a third-party voice AI platform — began offering Grok Bot phone number integration through what it calls the Agent Phone Plan. Grok Bot phone capabilities already exist in the wild through a third-party integration, even if SpaceXSI has not formally shipped calling in the stable release. The regulatory questions about disclosure and recording apply to this integration too, and Bland AI's terms would layer on top of whatever SpaceXSI's terms eventually say.

## Trust Is Already a Problem

The timing of this announcement is not great for AI voice technology broadly. Public trust in AI-generated voice calls is low — multiple surveys through 2025 and 2026 have found that a significant majority of consumers express distrust of AI systems that initiate voice communication.

A pending lawsuit about AI call recording, a regulatory ruling that explicitly covers AI voice calls, and a consumer base that is broadly skeptical of the technology create an environment where announcing a calling feature without documentation looks like a strategic mistake, not just a compliance oversight.

## What "Announced but Undocumented" Means for Builders

If you are building on top of Grok Bot's capabilities — through the API, through Bland AI's integration, or through any other channel — the lack of documentation creates real problems.

You cannot tell users which countries support calling. You cannot explain the disclosure policy because there is no public disclosure policy. You cannot point to a terms-of-service section that covers calling because one does not appear to exist. You are building on a feature that the CEO says exists but the company has not formally shipped.

Calling is different from most features because it involves regulations with real enforcement mechanisms. The TCPA covers AI-generated voices, and violations carry consequences that go well beyond the usual "the API might change" concern. Building on an undocumented calling feature without understanding the compliance requirements is a material risk.

## The Changelog Gap Is the Story

The feature itself — an AI that calls businesses and takes notes for you — is genuinely useful. If Grok Bot can navigate a phone tree, wait on hold, and summarize the outcome, that saves real time. Google proved the concept with Duplex eight years ago.

But the story here is not whether the feature works. The story is the gap between announcement and documentation. Musk says it exists. The changelog says nothing. The documentation says nothing. The regulatory questions have no public answers.

That gap matters because phone calls are not like most AI features. When an AI sends a message for you, the recipient can read it at their pace and decide how to respond. When an AI calls someone, it occupies their attention in real time, with no written record the recipient can review first. The bar for responsible deployment should be higher, not lower.

<Callout type="tip">
What to watch for: an entry in the Grok Bot changelog at x.ai/changelog/bot that mentions calling, a documentation page at docs.x.ai covering disclosure and recording, and a supported-countries list. Until those appear, the feature's status is "announced by CEO, not officially shipped."
</Callout>

## What Comes Next

SpaceXSI will almost certainly ship calling to stable. The prerelease builds are too far along for this to be vaporware. The question is whether the stable release comes with the documentation, disclosure policies, and compliance infrastructure that phone calling requires.

The optimistic scenario: SpaceXSI publishes a calling page in the docs, builds in disclosure at the start of every call, addresses recording in the terms of service, and rolls out by country based on local regulations. That would put them roughly on par with where Google is today with Duplex.

The pessimistic scenario: the feature appears in a changelog entry with a one-line description, no documentation page ships, disclosure is left to the AI's judgment, and the first anyone hears about the recording policy is when a lawsuit surfaces it.

Given that the calling feature was announced by the CEO before any changelog entry or documentation appeared — and that the prerelease builds are already far ahead of the stable release — the pessimistic scenario seems more likely than the optimistic one.

If you are a Grok Bot user, the advice is simple: wait for the changelog entry and the documentation page. If you are a builder integrating Grok Bot, the advice is simpler: do not ship calling integrations to production until SpaceXSI publishes the compliance documentation.
