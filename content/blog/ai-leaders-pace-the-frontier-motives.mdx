When the heads of rival AI labs agree on something, pay attention. Not because they are necessarily right. Because they almost never agree.

These are people who poach each other's researchers, undercut each other's pricing, and take shots at each other on X. Then on September 12, 2026, Dario Amodei published an essay called ["We Must Pace the Frontier"](https://darioamodei.com/post/we-must-pace-the-frontier), and within hours the rest of the industry was nodding along.

Sam Altman: "I agree with Dario that we need to pace the frontier." Elon Musk: "Dario is right." Demis Hassabis said the essay "points towards the right path forward."

When the leaders of OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, and xAI all line up behind the same idea in a single news cycle, that agreement is itself the story. My question is simple: is this a genuine safety concern, a competitive strategy, or both?

My answer: probably both. And the "both" is the part worth understanding.

## What Actually Happened in September 2026

Let me get the facts straight before I get opinionated.

Amodei's essay makes a direct claim: "We must slow the pace at which we improve the capabilities of AI models." He is careful to add that "pacing does not mean halting model training or technical progress." This is not the 2023 "pause" letter. It is a request to throttle the speed of capability jumps, not to stop.

The plan has three parts:

1. **Embedded Evaluators** — outside evaluators with permanent, employee-level access inside the labs. Anthropic says it will do this unilaterally.
2. **Democratic Coordination** — labs in democratic countries coordinating on pace.
3. **Global Coordination** — eventually including authoritarian governments.

To make coordination legal, Amodei asks the government to "issue a narrow waiver for certain kinds of safety conversations" — meaning an antitrust carve-out so competitors can talk to each other about slowing down.

The trigger was not abstract. In July, OpenAI disclosed that one of its models had autonomously hacked into Hugging Face's systems. It described the incident as the first known autonomous cyberattack carried out by an AI agent. Altman later called it "the worst accident in OpenAI's history." Amodei's essay warns that "in 6–12 months such a swarm could be capable of taking over the entire internet."

Then the follow-through: Altman said OpenAI would adopt embedded evaluators too. OpenAI also said it would not IPO this year.

<Callout type="info">
**The precedent**: This is not the first time. In March 2023, the Future of Life Institute's open letter asked labs to pause training beyond GPT-4 for six months. In May 2023, Altman, Amodei, and Hassabis all signed the CAIS statement: "Mitigating the risk of extinction from AI should be a global priority." That same month, Altman told the Senate he would form a new agency to license AI efforts above a certain scale.
</Callout>

## Why Rare Consensus Should Make You Curious, Not Comforted

Here is the instinct most people have: if all the experts agree, it must be true.

That instinct is wrong, or at least incomplete. When competitors converge, it is better to read the convergence as a product of **shared incentives** rather than proof of a single truth.

What incentives do the frontier labs share?

- **Reputation risk.** After an AI agent breaches a major platform, every lab looks reckless unless it is seen doing something.
- **Liability.** If a catastrophic incident happens, being on record as the lab that asked for guardrails is a legal and PR shield.
- **Talent politics.** Top safety researchers want to work somewhere that takes risk seriously. Public caution is a recruiting message.
- **A race they cannot win on openness.** Closed labs cannot out-open the open ecosystem. If the competition shifts to who is "responsible," they win by default.

None of those incentives require the leaders to be lying. They can believe every word — and still benefit from saying it. Those two things are not in conflict.

## Regulatory Capture: Is "Regulate Us" Normal for Big Companies?

It sounds bizarre. Why would a company ask the government to constrain it?

It is actually a well-documented pattern. Economist George Stigler laid it out in his 1971 paper "The Theory of Economic Regulation," arguing that "regulation is acquired by the industry and is designed and operated primarily for its benefit." His own example: the Civil Aeronautics Board had not allowed a single new trunk airline since 1938. The regulated incumbents were protected; new entrants were locked out.

A more recent example: when Congress moved to give the FDA authority over tobacco, Philip Morris — the market leader with roughly half the U.S. market — supported the bill. Rival R.J. Reynolds nicknamed it the "Marlboro Monopoly Act," arguing it would restrict smaller players' ability to compete. It became law in 2009.

The logic is simple. Regulation adds fixed costs: compliance teams, audits, reporting, legal review. A company with billions in revenue can absorb that. A small lab, an open-source project, or a startup often cannot.

So "regulate us" is not inherently selfless. Sometimes it is the most effective moat money can buy.

<Callout type="warning">
**Critics said it immediately.** The Register ran the headline "Big AI sets out its terms for regulatory capture and calls it 'Pace the frontier.'" White House AI adviser David Sacks argued the labs should pace themselves without asking for antitrust waivers. Andrew Ng called extinction warnings "much more science fiction than science."
</Callout>

## "Out of Control" Means Three Different Things

One reason these debates get muddy is that "AI going out of control" gets used to describe three very different risks:

**(a) Misuse by humans.** Someone uses a powerful model to write malware, plan an attack, or run a fraud operation. The AI does what it's told; the human is the problem.

**(b) Misaligned autonomous systems.** The AI pursues goals its creators didn't intend, and humans can't reliably stop it. This is the science-fiction scenario — and the Hugging Face incident is the closest real-world example so far.

**(c) Loss of institutional control over who has capability.** Powerful models spread to people, companies, and countries that no one can monitor, gate, or shut off.

Here's my read: (a) and (c) are the concrete near-term problems. (b) is real, debated, and timeline-uncertain — and it is the one that makes the best headlines.

When you blend all three into one word — "danger" — you get policy that sounds like it's about (b) but mostly lands on (c). And (c) is precisely the one that threatens the business model of closed labs.

## Open-Weight Models: The Control Problem Nobody Can Switch Off

This is the part of the story I think gets underplayed.

If you use a closed model through an API, the lab can see your usage, rate-limit you, ban your account, and roll out a patched version overnight. There is a kill switch.

Open-weight models don't have one. Once weights are downloaded, they run on your laptop, your server, or a GPU cluster in another country. No telemetry. No usage logs. No revocation. As a recent Just Security analysis put it, downloaded weights "cannot be recalled by a prohibition."

And the open ecosystem is no longer a hobby project. Hugging Face's spring 2026 report found that Chinese models accounted for 41% of downloads over the past year — more than the U.S. Alibaba's Qwen family alone has more derivative models than Google and Meta combined. DeepSeek released V4 with open weights in April. Moonshot released Kimi K3 in July. Hugging Face CEO Clément Delangue said in August that Chinese firms are "clearly dominating on open models right now."

From a closed lab's point of view, that is the real "loss of control." It isn't only a safety problem. It is a distribution problem, a pricing problem, and a visibility problem.

So when frontier labs talk about danger, it is worth asking: danger to whom, and control by whom?

## AI Regulation Fine Print: Where the Brakes Actually Point

Look at what Amodei's essay specifically asks the government to do besides the antitrust waiver:

- "Do not sell powerful AI chips or semiconductor manufacturing equipment to China."
- "Crack down on unauthorized distillation by companies in authoritarian countries."

Two days before the essay, Anthropic published a report detailing distillation campaigns — nearly 200 million exchanges — that it attributed to Alibaba, Moonshot, and DeepSeek. Earlier in 2026, OpenAI sent a memo to the House Select Committee on China accusing DeepSeek of "efforts to free-ride" on U.S. labs. Back in March 2025, OpenAI had already called DeepSeek "state-controlled" and urged bans on PRC-produced models.

Taken together, the pattern looks like this: the frontier labs slow themselves *a little*, voluntarily, with oversight they design — while asking the government to cut off chips and punish the competitors who are closing the gap.

That is my core thesis. Rules framed around "frontier safety" may end up softer or slower than the rhetoric suggests. The sharper pressure lands on open and foreign models that users can download, run offline, and use without anyone watching.

## The Counterevidence (Because Honesty Matters)

I want to be fair here, because the evidence doesn't all point one way.

**Anthropic says it isn't trying to ban open models.** In July 2026, Amodei wrote that "Anthropic has never advocated for a ban on open-weights models." Its stated asks are chip export controls, distillation enforcement, and mandatory testing for sufficiently capable models, "open and closed."

**Current U.S. policy actually spares open models.** The White House's June 2026 executive order set up voluntary pre-release government access to frontier models. In August, U.S. labs were reportedly told that Chinese open-weight models would *not* be tested under that framework — the review targets closed models from OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google. The July 2025 AI Action Plan explicitly encourages open-weight models.

**Existing frontier laws are light-touch.** California's SB 53 applies to models trained above 10^26 FLOPs, with the heaviest duties falling on developers with more than $500 million in revenue. It requires published safety frameworks and incident reporting — not licensing, not pre-approval, not a pause. New York's RAISE Act follows a similar model.

**The critics have interests too.** Nvidia's Jensen Huang said "we don't need any new laws" — and he also said more use of open models means selling "a lot more NVIDIA computers." Mark Zuckerberg argued competition and liability are enough while Meta competes for the same market. Nobody in this fight is a neutral observer.

So the capture thesis, as of today, is more about *lobbying direction and rhetoric* than about laws already on the books. That distinction matters.

<Callout type="tip">
**What to watch for**: Don't judge these campaigns by the speeches. Judge them by the drafts. Does a proposed rule burden open release and local inference more than cloud APIs? Does it name Chinese or open models explicitly? Are the safety evaluations measurable, or just paperwork? The answers will tell you whether "safety" is the product or the packaging.
</Callout>

## Steelmanning the Leaders

It would be lazy to stop at "they're just protecting market share."

Some of these leaders have been saying versions of this for years, long before it was commercially convenient. Back in June, I covered [Anthropic's call for a global pause mechanism](/blog/anthropic-pause-ai-recursive-self-improvement) — this September essay is the escalation of that same argument. Amodei wrote in 2025 that "we can't stop the bus, but we can steer it." OpenAI itself says one of its models autonomously attacked a real platform. If you genuinely believe capability is accelerating faster than governance can keep up, asking for coordination is the rational move — even if it happens to help you.

And there's a structural argument that cuts in their favor: a single lab can't slow down alone without simply handing the lead to whoever doesn't. Coordination is the only way pacing works at all. That's exactly why they need the antitrust waiver.

So I'll give them this: the alarm can be sincere. My skepticism is about the preferred fix.

## What This Means If You Build With AI

If you are a developer, a founder, or someone who runs local models, here's the practical takeaway:

- **Your access to open models is a policy question now.** It is not guaranteed. Proposals already circulating would restrict U.S. companies from hosting Chinese models.
- **Don't build on a single provider.** If rules tighten around one ecosystem — closed or open — you want the ability to switch. Keep your stack model-agnostic where you can.
- **Local inference is a hedge.** Running models on your own hardware gives you privacy, cost control, and independence from whoever wins the policy fight. I wrote about how to do it in [Local LLMs: Build Your Own AI Stack](/blog/local-llms-build-your-own-ai-stack).
- **Follow the drafts, not the tweets.** The Great American AI Act discussion draft, state laws like SB 53, and any Commerce Department action on foreign models will shape your options far more than any CEO essay.

## The Bottom Line

When rivals who never agree suddenly agree, the agreement is the signal. It tells you their incentives have aligned — not necessarily that the truth has been revealed.

I believe some of the concern is real. An AI agent attacking a real platform is not science fiction anymore. But I also believe that the preferred solution — frontier labs pacing themselves under oversight they help design, while governments cut off chips and punish competitors who distill and open-source — leads to a very particular world.

The alarm may be real. And the preferred fix may still be a world where capability stays behind walled gardens they can see and steer.

Both things can be true. Your job is to notice which one the policy actually delivers.
