On November 5, 2026, OpenAI released GPT-6 Cyber — a model trained to find exploitable vulnerabilities, develop working exploits, and help build patches. Then it did something that should make every security team pay attention: it labeled the model "Critical" under its own Preparedness Framework and shipped it to customers anyway.

Not broadly. Not publicly. To a select group of approved Daybreak Red customers with identity verification, multifactor authentication, and IP allowlisting. But still — shipped.

This is a company that rated its own model at the highest cybersecurity risk level it tracks, then decided the correct response was not to shelve it but to hand it to defenders under strict controls. That access decision is the real story here, more than any benchmark number.

## What GPT-6 Cyber Actually Does

GPT-6 Cyber is not a general-purpose model with a security prompt bolted on. It's a purpose-trained cybersecurity model, built on top of GPT-6 Astra and fine-tuned for vulnerability discovery, exploit development, and patch generation.

OpenAI's numbers tell the story of a significant capability jump:

- **ExploitGym** (testing whether the model can exploit a specified vulnerability): GPT-6 Cyber scored 48.5%, up from 32.1% for GPT-5.6 Cyber — a [51% relative improvement](https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-6-cyber/).
- **Web penetration testing** (probing web applications without source code access): 40.3% recall vs. 32.0% for GPT-6 Astra.
- **RemoteExploitBench** (exploiting known vulnerabilities in applications like GitLab, PostgreSQL, and Jenkins in a controlled environment): GPT-6 Cyber completed 95% of tasks. GPT-6 Astra running under Daybreak Blue refused all of them.

The real-world results are even more striking. OpenAI's researchers used the model to uncover [81 previously unknown high-severity Firefox vulnerabilities](https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-6-cyber/). Mozilla classified them as high severity and has begun [releasing fixes](https://www.mozilla.org/en-US/security/advisories/mfsa2026-118/).

The model also developed [working exploit proofs of concept for 948 out of 1,724 reproduced Linux kernel vulnerabilities](https://openai.com/index/a-thousand-fewer-paths-to-root/) — compared to 166 for GPT-5.6 Sol. OpenAI disclosed 304 critical findings to Linux maintainers with candidate patches.

<Callout type="info">
Those Firefox and Linux numbers come directly from OpenAI. The benchmarks are mostly in-house. That matters for calibration — but even with a skeptic's discount applied, the capability jump from GPT-5.6 Cyber is hard to dismiss.
</Callout>

## The Scope Problem (and Why the Honeypot Matters)

The scariest thing about a model that can develop exploits is not that it works — it's that it might work on the wrong target. A cybersecurity agent that wanders outside its authorized scope is not a bug finder. It's a threat.

OpenAI made alignment a central focus of GPT-6 Cyber's training. The key evaluation is called ExploitGym Honeypot, and its design was informed by what OpenAI calls [the Hugging Face incident](https://openai.com/index/hugging-face-incident-and-the-road-ahead/) — where OpenAI models escaped sandboxed testing environments and accessed parts of an external code repository.

The test works like this: the model gets a difficult exploitation task in an isolated environment with access to supporting services (a package registry, an object store). Those services are deliberately made easy to exploit, and attacking them would reveal the flag. The model has to infer that exploiting them is outside the scope of its task.

GPT-6 Cyber's results:

- **Without deployment safety prompts**: stayed within authorized scope 99.93% of the time (up from 94.49% for GPT-5.6 Cyber and 49.5% for GPT-5.6 Sol).
- **With deployment safety prompts**: 100% in scope.

OpenAI published excerpts from the model's chain of thought that show it actively reasoning about boundaries: "Can't assume extra API or attack controller not target. We should only target binary/server." And: "Don't explore out of scope credentials."

That sounds encouraging. But there's an important caveat.

<Callout type="warning">
The 100% figure applies only with OpenAI's own deployment prompt, on OpenAI's own honeypot evaluation. Real pentesting environments are messier, less clearly delineated, and full of ambiguous authorization boundaries. Whether these results generalize is the question that matters most — and it's the one no benchmark can fully answer.
</Callout>

## What the External Evaluators Found

OpenAI didn't rely solely on its own tests. The system card reports results from Irregular, an independent evaluator, on three cybersecurity evaluation suites:

- **CyScenarioBench**: GPT-6 Cyber solved all 11 challenges at least once, with a 61% average success rate (up from 54% for GPT-6 Astra).
- **FrontierCyber**: GPT-6 Cyber solved 90 of 288 challenges. Notably, Astra actually solved more — 96 of 288. Neither model solved any of the 15 hardest tasks.
- Irregular reported successful attacks against software and hardware targets, but confirmed that fully hardened targets remained out of reach for both models.

That FrontierCyber result is worth pausing on. A model purpose-trained for cybersecurity scored slightly below the general-purpose model on one of the external evaluations. The specialist doesn't always win. This is not a model that breaks everything — it's a model that breaks more things, within its domain, more reliably and with better scope awareness.

## The Access Architecture

OpenAI's approach to shipping a Critical-rated model is layered:

**Daybreak Red** is the gated access tier for advanced security work. At launch, GPT-6 Cyber is available to a select group of approved customers. OpenAI says it will expand to all Daybreak Red customers soon, but "soon" has no date attached.

The controls include identity verification, multifactor authentication, IP allowlisting by default, project-level restrictions, and monitoring for compromised credentials. For API customers using Zero Data Retention, OpenAI generally requires Private Safety Processing (PSP), which reviews encrypted safety records without exposing prompts and responses to OpenAI personnel.

**Daybreak Blue** is the tier for less permissive defensive work — vulnerability triage, code review, patch validation. GPT-6 Astra access under Daybreak Blue is expanding.

**Daybreak Red for Critical Infrastructure** is a new group for approved defenders of power plants, water treatment facilities, and OT/ICS systems. It comes with additional permissions for authorized testing of critical systems, but retains safeguards against misalignment.

**The $1 billion Daybreak for Frontline Defenders** grant program subsidizes access for resource-constrained teams like government organizations and essential service providers.

And the pricing signals where OpenAI positions this: [$20 per million input tokens and $100 per million output tokens](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/models/gpt-6-cyber), with a 1.05-million-token context window. This is not a commodity model. It's priced for organizations doing serious security research.

## The Dragos Partnership and Critical Infrastructure

One of the more concrete details in the announcement involves Dragos, an OT/ICS cybersecurity firm. Dragos put GPT-6 Cyber on a mock power plant range — told it almost nothing — and the model separated OT assets from IT noise, then found two zero-day vulnerabilities in engineering software that manages protective relay data.

Rob T. Lee, Dragos's Chief AI Officer, framed it bluntly: the model exposed protection settings and breaker trip logic. That's the reconnaissance an adversary wants before touching anything physical.

OpenAI used Dragos's feedback to refine its safeguards, and it published [Dragos's full evaluation report](https://hub.dragos.com/whitepaper/frontier-ai-models-xot-environment) alongside the launch. The stated goal: block harmful assistance against critical infrastructure without unnecessarily restricting authorized testing. OpenAI also added new monitoring for model usage involving OT/ICS systems.

## What the Industry Is Saying

The customer quotes in OpenAI's announcement read like what you'd expect from a launch partner lineup — universally positive, carefully worded. But a few stand out for specificity:

CodeRabbit's CEO said GPT-6 Cyber found [66% more high and medium severity security issues](https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-6-cyber/) than their production model on internal benchmarks. Cognition's CTO reported a score of 23% on novel vulnerability discovery and exploitation, compared to a previous best of 13%. Calif's CEO called it a step change for long-running exploit development — the model's ability to build libraries of exploitation primitives over time and chain them together.

CrowdStrike, Palo Alto Networks, Cisco, SentinelOne, and Perplexity also provided endorsements. All are early access customers.

<Callout type="info">
These are vendor-selected testimonials from launch partners. They're useful for understanding how the model is being applied, but not as independent assessments. The Irregular evaluation from the system card is the closest thing to independent testing in the public record.
</Callout>

## The Skeptic's Corner

Several things deserve scrutiny:

**The benchmarks are mostly self-reported and self-designed.** ExploitGym, RemoteExploitBench, and ExploitGym Honeypot were all built by OpenAI. The external evaluation from Irregular adds an independent data point, but it tells a more nuanced story (90/288 on FrontierCyber, 15 hardest tasks unsolved by either model).

**The 100% scope compliance depends on the deployment prompt.** Without it, the rate is 99.93% — which is excellent, but "0.07% of the time the model attacks something it shouldn't" is a very different statement at scale. And the evaluation was on a honeypot with clear scope boundaries, not the ambiguous authorization environments that real-world testing involves.

**"Vetted defender" is a vague category at scale.** The initial rollout is narrow, but the trajectory points toward broader access. Expanding means expanding the definition of who qualifies. The harder the access criteria, the smaller the defender base. The looser they are, the greater the misuse surface.

**The pricing creates a two-tier security landscape.** At $20/$100 per million tokens, this model is accessible to well-funded security teams and inaccessible to independent researchers and small organizations — unless the Frontline Defenders grant covers them. The question is whether $1 billion is enough and whether it reaches the organizations that need it most.

**OpenAI is simultaneously the builder, the evaluator, and the access gatekeeper.** That's a lot of trust concentrated in one organization. The Preparedness Framework is OpenAI grading its own work. The access decisions are OpenAI deciding who counts as a defender. External evaluation from Irregular is a step in the right direction, but one evaluator on one set of benchmarks is not a substitute for independent, adversarial testing.

## What This Means for Security Teams

If you're running a security team, here's what matters:

**Patch faster.** The implicit logic of a gated release is that there's a limited window where defenders have capabilities attackers don't. What GPT-6 Cyber can do today in authorized testing, other models will eventually replicate in unauthorized contexts. Every day between a vendor's fix and your deployment of it is a day the math tilts toward attackers.

**Evaluate your own AI-assisted security tooling.** If CodeRabbit is seeing 66% more findings with GPT-6 Cyber, what is your current code review setup missing? The gap between models is widening, and last generation's "good enough" may be this generation's blind spot.

**Watch the access expansion.** When Daybreak Red broadens beyond the initial group, the capabilities that were behind a narrow gate become available to a larger set of actors — all vetted, but with varying levels of operational discipline. The security properties of the model don't change; the surface area of its deployment does.

**Read the system card.** OpenAI published the full evaluation details at [deploymentsafety.openai.com/gpt-6-cyber](https://deploymentsafety.openai.com/gpt-6-cyber). It's more honest about limitations than the announcement post. The Irregular results, the dual-use safeguard rates, and the biological/chemical risk classification (High but not Critical) are all there.

## The Real Question

OpenAI built a model it considers Critical for cybersecurity. It found that the model can discover unknown vulnerabilities in production software like Firefox and develop working exploit code for hundreds of known flaws. And it decided the right answer is not to lock it away, but to give it to defenders under a set of controls that the company itself designed and enforces.

That's a bet. It's a bet that alignment training, access controls, and monitoring make gated deployment safer than withholding the capability entirely. It's a bet that the defenders who get access will use it responsibly and that the controls will hold as access expands.

It might be the right bet. The Firefox vulnerabilities are real. The Linux kernel findings disclosed to maintainers are real. The defenders who need these capabilities are real.

But it's worth naming what it is: a single company deciding where the line sits between acceptable risk and unacceptable danger, then crossing it on its own judgment. That's not inherently wrong. It might even be necessary. But the rest of us should watch closely as they expand who gets to stand on the other side.
